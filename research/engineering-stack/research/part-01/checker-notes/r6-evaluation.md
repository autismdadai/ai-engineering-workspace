## 5. Answers to the 7 questions

### Q1. Semantic entropy (Farquhar, Kossen, Kuhn and Gal, Nature 630:625-630, 2024)

**Takeaway.** The audited paper's description and formula are right in form. It leaves out three things the Nature paper states: how cluster probability is estimated (summed sequence probabilities, or counts in the discrete variant), the cost (10 samples plus entailment calls) and the blind spot (errors a model makes consistently).

**Findings.** All from https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07421-0 (DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07421-0, peer-reviewed), read through the fetch relay (first 100,000 of 101,519 characters).
- Method (Methods > Generating a set of answers; Clustering by semantic equivalence; Entailment estimator; Extended Data Fig. 1): for each question, sample M = 10 answers (temperature 1, nucleus sampling 0.9, top-K 50), plus one low-temperature (0.1) answer used as the "best" answer when checking accuracy. Two answers share a meaning if each entails the other with the question as context. Entailment is judged by GPT-3.5 (Turbo 1106) or DeBERTa-large-MNLI (the relay says the authors settled on GPT-3.5; the text says GPT-4 could also be used). Each new answer is compared with the first answer of each existing cluster.
- Formula (Methods > Computing the semantic entropy; Eq. 2 and Eq. 5 as relayed): SE(x) is approximately minus the sum over clusters of P(C_i given x) times log P(C_i given x). P(cluster) is the sum, over sampled sequences s in the cluster, of P(s given x), the product of token probabilities, normalised so cluster probabilities sum to 1. The text ties that normalisation to length normalisation, which is defined as the mean token log-probability and is stated for the naive-entropy baseline.
- Discrete variant (Methods): P(C_i) is approximated by the fraction of the M samples in cluster i, ignoring token probabilities. It is used when probabilities are unavailable; all GPT-4 results, including the biography experiments, use it because GPT-4 did not expose probabilities or hidden states then. The authors report it performs similarly (no number in the text).
- Results, sentence-length answers (Results > Detecting confabulations in QA and math; Fig. 2): 6 models (Falcon Instruct 7B and 40B; LLaMA 2 Chat 7B, 13B, 70B; Mistral Instruct 7B) x 5 datasets (TriviaQA, SQuAD 1.1, BioASQ, NQ-Open, SVAMP) = 30 combinations. Average AUROC: semantic entropy 0.790, naive entropy 0.691, P(True) 0.698, embedding regression 0.687. SE stayed between 0.78 and 0.81 across model families and sizes. AURAC is plotted; the text gives no values. Answers averaged 96 +/- 70 characters.
- Results, paragraph-length (Results > Detecting confabulations in biographies; Fig. 3): GPT-4 (v.0613), 21 individuals, 150 factual claims of which 45 incorrect (as relayed). Discrete SE beat the self-check and P(True) baselines on AUROC and AURAC; P(True) edges ahead only after the top 20% of answers are rejected. AUROC values are only in Fig. 3 (not read).
- Stated limits (Main text; Discussion): the method does not guarantee factuality. It targets confabulations (wrong and arbitrary answers), not errors a model makes consistently, for instance "consistently wrong as a result of being trained on erroneous data". The authors call that a different mechanism that needs separate handling. Other limits relayed: the scores are a lower bound; clustering can be overly sensitive (Table 1); the question-generation step of the biography pipeline is the main source of errors.

**Gaps.** Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 values; discrete-SE AUROC on the sentence-length tasks; the references block. Equation numbers and "settled on GPT-3.5" are relay-level, not seen raw.

### Q2. Semantic entropy probes (Kossen et al. 2024, arXiv 2406.15927)

**Takeaway.** SEPs are a cheap stand-in for semantic entropy: a trained probe reads the model's hidden states from one generation and predicts the semantic-entropy value, so there is no repeated sampling or entailment judging at test time. The price is that you need hidden-state access and a training step.

**Findings.** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15927 (arXiv 2406.15927v1, preprint; authors Kossen, Han, Razzak, Schut, Malik, Gal; only v1 listed, 2024-06-22; no venue on the abs page; abstract relayed as a summary).
- The abstract says SEPs "directly approximate SE from the hidden states of a single generation".
- Sampling-based SE multiplies compute by 5 to 10 (abstract); SEPs cut that overhead to almost zero because only one generation is needed at test time.
- The abstract reports that SEPs keep high hallucination-detection performance and generalize better out of distribution than probes that predict accuracy directly; ablations look at which token positions and layers carry the signal. It calls them simple to train.
- Differences from Nature's method: sampling-based SE needs about 10 samples plus an entailment judge but can run black-box in its discrete form; SEPs need white-box access to hidden states and a trained probe (my inference from the abstract) and no samples at test time.

**Gaps.** The paper body was not opened: models, datasets, AUROC values, training targets, probe design. No source I opened tests SEPs or SE in a RAG pipeline.

### Q5. DSPy docs, MIPROv2 and GEPA

**Takeaway.** The name and the Bayesian claims are confirmed by DSPy's own docs. GEPA is in DSPy. GEPA's headline figures are real but are best-case margins, and its Pareto front is per validation instance.

**Findings.**
- MIPROv2 (https://dspy.ai/current/api/optimizers/MIPROv2/, official docs): the name is given as "Multiprompt Instruction PRoposal Optimizer Version 2". It uses Bayesian optimization to find the best combination of instruction candidates and few-shot candidates for each predictor, optimizing both jointly. The MIPRO paper specifies the surrogate as Optuna's multivariate TPE over latent categorical variables (instruction candidates and bootstrapped demonstration sets per module; Sec. 3.2, Sec. 4.3, App. F.5 as relayed).
- GEPA in DSPy (https://dspy.ai/current/api/optimizers/GEPA/overview/): yes, dspy.GEPA ("Genetic-Pareto") under API Reference > Optimizers; engine from the separate gepa package. It captures execution traces, isolates the part belonging to each predictor, reflects in natural language and proposes new instructions; metrics may return a score plus text feedback. Exactly one of auto, max_full_evals or max_metric_calls must be set; reflection_lm is required unless a custom proposer is supplied. The page says textual feedback lets GEPA find strong prompts in very few rollouts and gives no numbers.
- GEPA paper (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.19457v2 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.19457; arXiv 2507.19457; Agrawal et al., 17 authors including Opsahl-Ong, Singhvi, Khattab and Zaharia; v1 2025-07-25, v2 2026-02-14; PDF page headers "Accepted at ICLR 2026 (Oral)"). Title: "GEPA: Reflective Prompt Evolution Can Outperform Reinforcement Learning".
- Headline figures (abstract, v2; same numbers in the abs-page relay, the PDF relay and the W22 blog): GEPA beats GRPO by 6% on average and up to 20% with up to 35x fewer rollouts; beats MIPROv2 by over 10%, including +12% accuracy on AIME-2025; six tasks.
- Model scope (Tables 1 and 2, as relayed): Qwen3 8B (open; GRPO comparison run) and GPT-4.1 mini (proprietary; no GRPO). Benchmarks: HotpotQA, IFBench, HoVer, PUPA, AIME-2025, LiveBench-Math. Aggregates: Qwen3 8B baseline 45.23, GRPO 48.91, MIPROv2 47.84, GEPA 54.85, GEPA+Merge 52.40; GPT-4.1 mini baseline 53.03, MIPROv2 58.67, GEPA 65.22, GEPA+Merge 66.36.
- How the "%" reads: score points. 6% = 5.94 (54.85 vs 48.91, Qwen3 8B). "Up to 20%" = HotpotQA 62.33 vs 43.33, 19.0 points (the intro says 20, Observation 1 says 19). 35x = 24,000 / 678 on IFBench (GEPA reached its IFBench optimum, 38.61, after 678 rollouts; it used 3,593 in total). "Over 10%" over MIPROv2 = best single-benchmark margins of 10.3 (Qwen3 8B) and 11.1 (GPT-4.1 mini); aggregate gaps are about 7.0 and 6.6 points. +12% on AIME-2025 = 32.00 vs 20.00 (Qwen3 8B); on GPT-4.1 mini the AIME gap is 59.33 vs 51.33. On Qwen3 8B, GRPO beat GEPA on AIME-2025 (38.00 vs 32.00).
- Rollouts: GRPO used 24,000 per benchmark. GEPA's totals on Qwen3 8B: HotpotQA 6,871; IFBench 3,593; HoVer 7,051; PUPA 2,426; AIME 1,839; LiveBench-Math 1,839. GEPA needed 243, 402, 330, 1,143, 1,179 and 306 rollouts to match GRPO's best validation score (task order not stated), up to 78x per Observation 1. The authors note most of GEPA's budget goes to validation rollouts.
- Pareto front (Sec. 3.1, Algorithm 2, Fig. 4 right, as relayed): for each instance in the Pareto set D_pareto (the relay calls it the validation set), record the best score and the candidates that reach it; drop strictly dominated candidates; weight each remaining candidate by the number of instances it still leads; sample the next parent in proportion. The DSPy docs describe the same idea (candidates best on at least one evaluation instance, sampled by coverage) and add an optional frontier_type (instance, objective, hybrid, cartesian). The assignment's wording "per training instance" differs slightly: the relay says validation instances. Not resolved.
- Stated limits (as relayed): validation rollouts dominate cost; merge hyperparameters were fixed across models (GEPA+Merge fell on Qwen3 8B IFBench, 28.23 vs 38.61); inference-time search results are early.

**Gaps.** The v1 abstract was not opened, so what changed in v2 is unknown. No independent replication opened. ICLR status rests on the PDF header and two blogs, not the conference site.

### Q6. LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al.), GPT-4 availability, self-preference

**Takeaway.** Zheng et al. support "a strong judge can match human agreement", but they also measured biases the audited paper omits. The original GPT-4 snapshots are mostly gone: gpt-4-0314 shut down in March 2026, and gpt-4 / gpt-4-0613 are scheduled to shut down on 2026-10-23. Later work supports the self-preference worry.

**Findings.**
- Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (venue per paper text), arXiv 2306.05685v4, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685.
  - Agreement (abstract; Table 5; Table 6): the abstract claims over 80% agreement between GPT-4 judge and human preferences, the same level as between humans. MT-bench pairwise, GPT-4 vs humans: 66% (S1, ties included) and 85% (S2, ties excluded) on both turns; single-answer grading 60% / 85% (turn 1) and 59% / 84% (turn 2); human vs human 63% / 81% (turn 1) and 67% / 82% (turn 2). Chatbot Arena: GPT-4 pairwise 64% (S1) and 87% (S2); single-answer 60% and 85%.
  - Biases (Sec. 3.3): position bias, consistency under swapped order with the default prompt: GPT-4 65.0%, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8% (Table 2); Claude-v1 picked the first position 75.0% of the time. Verbosity, "repetitive list" attack on 23 answers: failure rate Claude-v1 91.3%, GPT-3.5 91.3%, GPT-4 8.7% (Table 3). Self-enhancement: GPT-4 about 10% higher win rate for itself, Claude-v1 about 25%, GPT-3.5 none (Fig. 3b); the authors say the data cannot settle whether the bias exists. Math and reasoning: GPT-4 was misled by supplied answers when judging problems it could solve alone (Fig. 13 and 14).
  - Mitigations (Sec. 3.4): judge in both orders and call a tie if the verdicts differ; a few-shot judge lifts GPT-4 consistency from 65.0% to 77.5% (Claude-v1 23.8% to 63.7%, GPT-3.5 46.2% to 55.0%; Table 12) with agreement to humans similar to zero-shot (as relayed); chain-of-thought and reference-guided prompts cut math-grading failures from 14/20 to 6/20 to 3/20 (Table 4).
  - GPT-4 version: the main text names none; Appendix B says the case-study examples were generated by gpt-4-0314.
- OpenAI availability (https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations, official docs, newest entry 2026-10-01): gpt-4-0314 shut down 2026-03-26; gpt-4 and gpt-4-0613 under "Upcoming", shutdown 2026-10-23 (announced 2026-04-22). So on 2026-10-09 the page lists them as scheduled, not removed. I did not open the models page or call the API. See Section 3 for oddities in the relay.
- Self-preference: Panickssery, Bowman and Feng, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations", https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076 (arXiv 2404.13076, v1 2024-04-15, abstract relayed as a summary). Self-evaluation introduces self-preference bias: the evaluator scores its own outputs higher even when human annotators rate them equal. GPT-4 and Llama 2 show non-trivial accuracy at telling their own text from other LLMs' and humans' out of the box. Fine-tuning shows a linear correlation between self-recognition and the strength of self-preference, and controlled experiments suggest the link is causal rather than a confound. The authors argue this interferes with unbiased evaluation. Venue not on the abs page; "NeurIPS 2024" is from the assignment and unverified by me.
- Reading together: Zheng et al. (2023) saw self-enhancement as suggestive and unproven; Panickssery et al. (2024) is the later study that supplies mechanism-level evidence. I opened no other later studies.
