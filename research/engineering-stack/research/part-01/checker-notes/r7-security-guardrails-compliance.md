### Q5. Prompt-injection defense evidence and what it implies for input sanitization

**Takeaway.** Both papers argue against relying on input filtering. Nasr et al. (preprint, 2025-10-10) tested 12 published jailbreak and prompt-injection defenses with adaptive attacks and bypassed most of them, with attack success above 90% for most, although most had originally reported near-zero. Greshake et al. (preprint, 2023) showed indirect injection against real LLM-integrated applications and said effective mitigations were lacking.

**Cited findings**
- Nasr et al., "The Attacker Moves Second" (arXiv 2510.09023v1, cs.LG and cs.CR, 14 authors including Carlini and Tramèr): argues that testing defenses on static attack strings or weak optimization is flawed; the authors tuned gradient-descent, reinforcement-learning, random-search and human-guided attacks; most of 12 defenses fell at above 90% attack success; no venue shown on the page. Source: [arXiv 2510.09023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09023)
- Greshake et al., "Not what you've signed up for" (arXiv 2302.12173v2, submitted 2023-02-23, v2 2023-05-05): LLM-integrated applications blur data and instructions; indirect injection plants instructions in content the application retrieves; demonstrated on Bing Chat (GPT-4) and code-completion engines plus synthetic GPT-4 apps; risks include data theft and worm-like spread; abstract says effective mitigations are currently lacking; venue not shown on the page. Source: [arXiv 2302.12173](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173)
- OWASP 2026 (as read) keeps filtering but narrower, notes semantic filters can be evaded, and puts weight on least privilege, deterministic policy checks in application code, human confirmation and testing against adaptive attackers. Source: [2026 PDF](https://genai.owasp.org/download/56857/?tmstv=1785822482)

**Inferences**
- Input sanitization is a speed bump, not a boundary. A filter that scores well on a fixed test set can still fail against an attacker who adapts to it, so published filter scores (including Meta's) should be read as best cases.
- The stronger controls are architectural: limit what a hijacked model can do, require human approval for privileged actions, and keep untrusted content separate. Indirect injection, which the paper omits, is the case sanitization of the user's own prompt does not cover.

**Gaps**
- The abstract lists no individual defenses. Background knowledge, not a source: the full paper is reported to cover prompting, training-based, filtering-model and other defense families; verify in the full text, which I did not open.
- Whether Greshake et al. appeared at a peer-reviewed venue is not shown on the page I opened.

### Q6. "Soft-prompt alignment (RLHF)": is it a correct term?

**Takeaway.** No. Soft prompts and RLHF are different techniques, and the paper's own example ("Do not be toxic" in a prompt) is neither.

**Cited findings**

Source: [Ouyang et al., arXiv 2203.02155](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)

**Inferences**
- Likely intended meaning: "the limits of instruction-only (system prompt) alignment and of training-based alignment such as RLHF". Suggested correction: name those two separately.

**Gaps**
- Neither abstract page showed a publication venue.
