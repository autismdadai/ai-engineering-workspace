## 5. Answers to the 8 questions

Q1 to Q6 and Q7 to Q8 answer the brief's questions in order. Each bullet names its source and class; the claim rows in section 1 hold the fuller numbers.

### Q1. RRF
- Cormack, Clarke, Buettcher, SIGIR 2009 (peer-reviewed conference paper; opened at https://cormack.uwaterloo.ca/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf). Formula in Sec. 1: RRFscore(d) = sum over rankings r of 1/(k + r(d)), with r(d) a position from 1..|D|. k = 60.
- How k was chosen: fixed during a pilot investigation (30 Wumpus Search configurations, TREC topics 351-400) and not altered afterward. Table 1 shows MAP by k from 0 to 500: about .2145 at k=60, peak about .2146 near k=70, .2098 at k=500. Authors say k=60 was near-optimal but the choice was not critical. So "typically 60" is the paper's own fixed value, picked on one pilot, not a universal constant.
- Compared against: Condorcet Fuse, CombMNZ, best single ranker (TREC 3, 5, 9 ad hoc, TREC 2004 Robust; Table 2) and learning-to-rank methods on LETOR 3 (ListNet, LGD, AdaRank-MAP, RankSVM, RankBoost; Table 3). Metric: MAP. Headline: about 4-5% better MAP on average than Condorcet, CombMNZ and the best single system in the TREC runs; RRF beat Condorcet in all 7 comparisons and CombMNZ in 6 of 7; on LETOR 3 RRF (.6051) beat every individual ranker but CombMNZ (.6107) was slightly higher (not significant); on TREC 9 the best single run beat RRF (it used a human in the loop).
- No normalization/training: Sec. 2 says RRF combines ranks without regard to arbitrary scores and needs no special voting algorithm; Sec. 1 frames unsupervised methods as needing no training examples. The paper says its reason why RRF works (diversity harnessed, k damps outlier high ranks) is a conjecture.
- Elasticsearch docs (official docs; https://www.elastic.co/docs/reference/elasticsearch/rest-apis/reciprocal-rank-fusion): pseudocode score += 1.0 / (k + rank(result(q), d)); rank starts from 1 (page says so); rank_constant default 60 (minimum 1; higher means lower-ranked docs have more influence); rank_window_size default = the request size, must be >= size and >= 1; page says "RRF requires no tuning"; first 100k of 121k characters read; no license tier stated in the part read. API example uses rank_constant 20 and window 50, so 60 is only the default.
- My own arithmetic (not a source): with k=60 and two rankers, a document at rank r in BOTH lists beats a document that is #1 in only one list when r < 62. The margin is at most 2.0x (both #1), 1.88x at rank 5, 1.52x at rank 20, about 1.0x at rank 60. With k=1 the single #1 wins. Redis's own example (#2 + #5 vs lone #1) gives 0.0315 vs 0.0164, ratio 1.92. So "agreement beats a lone first place" is right; "vastly" is not.

### Q2. ColBERT and ColBERTv2
- ColBERT (Khattab and Zaharia, SIGIR 2020, arXiv 2004.12832; opened https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.12832): MaxSim S(q,d) = sum over query embeddings of max over document embeddings of E_q_i . E_d_j (Eq. 3, Sec. 3.3). Embeddings are L2-normalized (Sec. 3.2) so the dot product is cosine similarity; the paper also evaluates squared L2 distance (used with FAISS in end-to-end retrieval, "ColBERT L2"). Dimension m=128. MS MARCO dev MRR@10: ColBERT re-ranking BM25 top-1000 = 34.9 at 61 ms vs BERT-base 34.7 at 10,700 ms (Table 1); end-to-end 36.0 (Table 2, L2). The paper's claim is parity with BERT-base cross-encoder accuracy at roughly 170x lower latency and 14,000x fewer FLOPs (V100, re-ranking). It does NOT report single-vector dense baselines such as DPR/ANCE in its tables.
- ColBERTv2 (Santhanam et al., arXiv 2112.01488v3, 10 Jul 2022; opened https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.01488; the arXiv text names no venue, the brief says NAACL 2022, not independently opened): adds residual compression of token vectors (20 or 36 bytes/vector vs 256 for ColBERT; MS MARCO index 154 GiB -> 16 or 25 GiB) and denoised supervision (cross-encoder distillation + hard negatives). Eq. 1 is the same MaxSim sum-max of Q_i . D_j^T.
- Results (Table 4, MS MARCO dev MRR@10, official 7k dev): ColBERTv2 39.7; ColBERT v1 36.0; DPR 31.1; ANCE 33.0; TAS-B 34.7; SPLADEv2 36.8; coCondenser 38.2; RocketQAv2 38.8. So the gain over classic single-vector dense models is 5.0 to 8.6 points, over the best strong single-vector systems 0.9 to 1.5 points.
- BEIR (Table 5a, nDCG@10, 13 datasets). First read: ColBERTv2 best on 6 (DBPedia, FiQA, NQ, NFCorpus, T-COVID, Quora), tied on SciFact, behind on 6 (HotpotQA, Touche, ArguAna, Climate-FEVER, FEVER, SCIDOCS), mostly to SPLADEv2. Second read (PDF opened twice): the "search tasks" block has 7 datasets and agrees with the first read: best on 5 (DBPedia 44.6, FiQA 35.6, NQ 56.2, NFCorpus 33.8, T-COVID 73.8), behind SPLADEv2 on HotpotQA (66.7 vs 68.4) and Touche v2 (26.3 vs 27.2); against the five single-vector systems listed (DPR-M, ANCE, MoDIR, TAS-B, RocketQAv2) it won all 31 pairwise comparisons. The two reads disagree on whether the other six sets (semantic-relatedness tasks) sit in the same table, which I could not resolve without re-opening, so the 13-dataset totals rest on the first read only. LoTTE (Table 5b, Success@5): ColBERTv2 best in all 12 rows, pooled search 71.6 vs BM25 48.3, ANCE 66.4, RocketQAv2 69.8, SPLADEv2 68.9; pooled forum 63.4 vs 47.2 / 55.7 / 57.7 / 60.1.
- Verdict on "vastly improves accuracy": direction right (consistent gains), strength overstated; gains are a few points against strong single-vector baselines and not uniform on BEIR. Authors list limits: English only, MS MARCO-trained, false negatives in IR data sets, harder training.

### Q3. Flash-MaxSim (arXiv 2605.29517)
- Exists (opened https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29517v3). Title "FLASH-MAXSIM: IO-Aware Fused Kernels for Late-Interaction Retrieval"; authors Roi Pony, Daniel Ezer, Adi Raz Goldfarb, Idan Friedman, Oshri Naparstek, Udi Barzelay, IBM Research Israel. Class: preprint (peer review not stated; reproducibility appendix mentions a branch named emnlp2026, which is not proof of acceptance). Dates and category are not shown on the HTML page, and the arXiv listing page was not opened (could not check (budget)), so whether a version newer than v3 exists is unknown.
- Claims: a fused GPU kernel computes the same MaxSim scores without building the full similarity tensor; inference peak memory 1.4-2.6x lower than chunked FP16 eager and 4.9-8.9x lower than unchunked eager (A100-80GB, ColPali scale, Tab. 2); training memory down about two orders of magnitude at operator level (Tab. 3); speed up to 3.8x (A100) and 4.7x (H100) vs naive PyTorch at ColPali shape, but only 0.97-1.95x against the best torch.compile configuration (Tab. 1, Sec. 5.2); nDCG@10 within 5e-4 of FP32 on BEIR and REAL-MM-RAG (Sec. 5.6); PyTorch 2.8, Triton 3.5/3.6.
- Eq. 1 defines MaxSim as sum over query tokens of max over document tokens of the inner product; Sec. 3.1 says token embeddings are L2-normalized (d=128). So it supports only the MaxSim formula (P5-08). It makes no accuracy comparison with single-vector retrieval, so it does not support P5-09. It says nothing about RRF, GraphRAG, hallucination or Neo4j. No text aimed at AI systems.

### Q4. Does RAG "drastically" reduce hallucination?
- Lewis et al. 2020 (arXiv 2005.11401v4, 12 Apr 2021; opened https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.11401; NeurIPS 2020 per the brief, venue not printed in the text I read): the only measured factuality result is a human evaluation on Jeopardy question generation (Sec. 4.3, Table 4): 452 pairs, RAG-Token vs BART; factuality "RAG better" 42.7% vs "BART better" 7.1% (both good 11.7%, both poor 17.7%, no majority 20.8%). Sec. 4.2 says RAG models "hallucinate less", offered as a qualitative observation. Broader Impact notes Wikipedia is never entirely factual. This is a head-to-head preference on one task against a closed-book BART, not a hallucination rate, and "drastically" is not in the paper.
- Magesh et al. (arXiv 2405.20362v1, 30 May 2024; preprint, the text says "Preprint. Under review."; Journal of Empirical Legal Studies 2025 per the brief, not verified; opened https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.20362): 202 hand-built legal queries (general research 80, jurisdiction or time-specific 70, false premise 22, factual recall 30), preregistered 22 Mar 2024. Hallucinated = incorrect or misgrounded (Sec. 4.3); accurate = correct and grounded; incomplete = refusal or ungrounded (Sec. 4.4). Results (Fig. 4, Sec. 6.1): Lexis+ AI accurate 65%, incomplete 18%, hallucination over 1 in 6 (about 17%); Westlaw AI-Assisted Research accurate 41% (42% in the introduction), incomplete 25%, hallucination about one third, which the paper says is nearly twice the rate of the other tools; Ask Practical Law AI accurate 19% (20% in footnote 18), incomplete 62%, hallucination over 1 in 6 (about 18%, derived by my extraction). The abstract states 17% to 33% across the tools. Conclusions: legal RAG can reduce hallucination compared with general-purpose AI (Sec. 6) but the tools have not eliminated it (Sec. 9). My extraction did not capture GPT-4 percentages. Limits (Sec. 7): three products, tested 22 Mar to 22 Apr 2024 (Lexis+ AI, Practical Law) and 23 to 27 May 2024 (Westlaw); 202 queries is not an unbiased sample; coder agreement Cohen's kappa 0.77.

### Q5. Dense retrieval and multi-hop
- MultiHop-RAG (Tang and Yang, arXiv 2401.15391v1, 27 Jan 2024; opened https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15391; preprint as read): 609 English news articles, 2,556 queries (inference 816, comparison 856, temporal 583, null 301). Best retrieval with a reranker: voyage-02 + bge-reranker-large MRR@10 0.586, Hits@10 0.7467, Hits@4 0.6625; without reranker best Hits@10 0.6718 (bge-large-en-v1.5). Answer accuracy (Table 6): GPT-4 0.56 with retrieved chunks vs 0.89 with ground-truth evidence; Claude-2.1 0.52 vs 0.56; ChatGPT 0.44 vs 0.57. Conclusion: existing RAG performs unsatisfactorily on multi-hop queries. The paper tests only dense embedding retrieval with cosine similarity; it does not run graph-based or BM25 comparisons.

### Q6. Microsoft GraphRAG (Edge et al.)
- Opened https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.16130 (v2, 19 Feb 2025, marked "Preprint. Under review"; preprint class). Builds: LLM extracts entities, relationships and claims from 600-token chunks; graph (entities = nodes, relationships = weighted edges); hierarchical Leiden communities; LLM-written community summaries; global answer = map-reduce over community summaries (partial answers with 0-100 helpfulness score, filtered, combined).
- Graph database: no Cypher; "Neo4j" appears once, only in the introduction, as one of several open-source libraries that offer GraphRAG extensions; the phrase "graph database" does not appear (the paper calls it a graph index).
- Evaluation: two corpora, podcast transcripts (about 1M tokens, 1,669 chunks) and news articles from the MultiHop-RAG data set (about 1.7M tokens, 3,197 chunks); 125 questions each, generated by an LLM from personas/tasks (activity-centered sensemaking, not from the corpus); criteria comprehensiveness, diversity, empowerment, plus directness as a control; LLM-as-judge head-to-head, five repeats averaged; baseline SS = vector RAG. Win rates over SS (Figure 2): comprehensiveness 72-83% podcast and 72-80% news; diversity 75-82% podcast and 62-71% news (TS and C0-C3 conditions); empowerment mixed (43-58%); directness favors SS (35-46% for graph conditions). Notable: the text-summarization map-reduce condition (TS) scored highest on comprehensiveness (83/80) but costs 100% of tokens; C0 root communities use about 97% fewer tokens. Limits: only two corpora of about 1M tokens, fabrication not measured, exact-string entity matching, judge model not captured by my extraction.

- Project status (official project repository): the releases page (https://github.com/microsoft/graphrag/releases) lists v3.1.2 as Latest, dated 21 Aug (year not printed; 2026 inferred from the page footer), then v3.1.1 (18 Jul), v3.1.0 (28 May), v3.0.9 (13 Apr) and v3.0.8 (27 Mar). The repository page (https://github.com/microsoft/graphrag) shows an MIT licence and a README note that the code is a demonstration and not an officially supported Microsoft offering, plus a warning that indexing can be expensive. It did not show a storage format, Python versions or any Neo4j or Cypher mention; release notes mention a CosmosTableProvider (v3.1.0), a parquet input reader (v3.0.9) and removal of the NetworkX dependency (v3.0.2).
- LazyGraphRAG (vendor blog, Microsoft Research, 25 Nov 2024, modified 6 Jun 2025; https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/): builds a noun-phrase concept graph with NLP at indexing and defers all LLM use to query time. Vendor-reported figures: indexing cost equal to vector RAG and 0.1% of full GraphRAG indexing; about 700x lower query cost than GraphRAG global search at comparable global-query quality; benchmark of 5,590 AP news articles and 100 synthetic queries (50 local, 50 global), LLM judge, authors' own evaluation with no independent check. Editor's note: integrated into Microsoft Discovery and Azure Local (public preview); the post does not say it is in the open-source library. Whether it is in the library as of Oct 2026: could not check (budget).
- Not reached (could not check (budget)): the GraphRAG documentation site, any independent comparison of GraphRAG with vector RAG, and the judge model named in Edge et al.

### Q7. Neo4j's GraphRAG approach, and Cypher versus ISO GQL
- neo4j-graphrag (official docs: https://neo4j.com/docs/neo4j-graphrag-python/current/ and https://neo4j.com/docs/neo4j-graphrag-python/current/user_guide_rag.html) is a first-party package, imported as neo4j_graphrag, described as the renamed continuation of the deprecated neo4j-genai. Retrievers: VectorRetriever (similarity search on a Neo4j vector index); VectorCypherRetriever (vector search, then a Cypher retrieval_query that traverses the graph from each hit); HybridRetriever (vector index plus full-text index); HybridCypherRetriever (hybrid search, then Cypher traversal); Text2CypherRetriever (an LLM writes the Cypher query from the question; no embedder needed); ToolsRetriever (an LLM chooses which retrievers to run); retrievers that match vectors held in Weaviate, Pinecone or Qdrant to Neo4j nodes; and custom retrievers by subclassing. The GraphRAG class takes a retriever, an LLM and an optional prompt template. Documented LLM providers include OpenAI, Azure OpenAI, VertexAI, Anthropic, MistralAI, Cohere and Ollama.
- Knowledge-graph building: the index page mentions knowledge-graph creation components (entity resolvers installed through extras) and an LLM-provider requirement for the "RAG and KG Builder Pipeline", but the pages I opened do not describe the extraction step or name SimpleKGPipeline. So "an LLM extracts entities into Neo4j" is plausible for Neo4j's stack but not shown by what I opened.
- Requirements: Python 3.10 to 3.14; Neo4j 5.18.1 or later (Aura 5.18.0 or later); Neo4j 2026.01 or later for SEARCH-clause in-index filtering. Package version and licence not shown: could not check (budget).
- ISO GQL (official standards catalogue, https://www.iso.org/standard/76120.html): ISO/IEC 39075:2024, "Information technology, Database languages, GQL", Edition 1, published April 2024, 610 pages. Scope: data structures and operations for property graphs (creating, accessing, querying, maintaining and controlling graphs and their data). Flagged "to be revised"; Corrigendum Cor 1:2026 published; a successor draft (CD 39075) is under development. The ISO page says nothing about Cypher or SQL/PGQ.
- Cypher's relation to GQL: could not check (budget); the fetch of Neo4j's GQL-conformance page failed on the session limit. Background knowledge, not a source: Cypher (through openCypher) was a major input to GQL, and Neo4j's Cypher is not identical to the standard.
