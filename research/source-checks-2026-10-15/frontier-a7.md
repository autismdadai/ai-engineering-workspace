## F01  A model misses its own mistakes more than the same mistake shown as someone else's (non-reasoning models)
- Source: Ken Tsui. "Self-Correction Bench: Uncovering and Addressing the Self-Correction Blind Spot in Large Language Models." arXiv 2507.02778. v1 July 2025 (month from the ID; day not shown), v2 4 Oct 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02778
- Evidence class: preprint. The page says "Preprint. Under review."
- Measured: the same error presented as the user's versus as the model's own earlier output, everything else equal. A score of 1 means a total blind spot (fixes the user's error, never its own). 14 open-source NON-reasoning models (for example Llama-4-Maverick, DeepSeek-V3-0324, Qwen2.5-72B, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen3-235B, phi-4, Mistral-Small-24B). Three datasets: SCLI5 (286 samples), GSM8K-SC (1,313), PRM800K-SC (448).
- Key numbers (all scoped to those 14 models and 3 datasets): average blind spot rate 64.5% (abstract). Appending the single word "Wait" after the wrong output cut the blind spot by 89.3%, averaged across models and datasets (abstract and body). The text says reasoning models show a small, even negative, blind spot (the page gives no number for that sentence). The authors link the gap to training data that has few correction examples.
- Not confirmed: a search summary quoted a "76.0% / 5,306 traces" fine-tuning result. I did not find it in the page text I read, so I do not use it.
- Related (opened): Chen, Su, Lin, Li, Chiang. "The Self-Correction Illusion: Role Relabeling Gates Explicit Error Flagging in Large Language Models." arXiv 2606.05976, v1 4 Jun 2026, v2 31 Jul 2026, preprint. Keeps the wrong claim word for word and changes only its message role (own thought block, user message, tool response, system memory block). Across 12 model-domain combinations (closed APIs, open models from 70B class down), moving the claim out of the model's own thought block raised the rate of explicit correction by 23 to 93 percentage points, significant in 10 of 12 settings. The authors conclude the failure is largely an artifact of role labeling. Model names not read. One group, one preprint.
- What an advanced user should do differently: do not ask a model to "check your answer" in the same thread. Hand the draft over as someone else's text ("a colleague wrote this; find the errors") or in a fresh chat, or use a reasoning model. This is supported in the tested settings only.
- Manual change: yes, small. Section 13.2 rests on 2023 models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4). Add that (a) a 2025 test on 14 non-reasoning models ties the failure to the error being the model's own, (b) reasoning models showed a small or negative blind spot, so the "not for correctness" rule is strongest for non-reasoning models, and (c) re-framing a draft as someone else's work is a cheap step that sits between self-critique and independent review (13.6). Point (c) is my inference from two preprints and does not replace independent review.

## F02  Reading the reasoning trace helps a monitor catch bad behavior, and current models cannot easily hide it
- Source: Melody Y. Guan et al. "Monitoring Monitorability." arXiv 2512.18311, v1 20 Dec 2025, cs.AI. https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18311
- Evidence class: preprint. No venue listed. The abstract page I read does not show affiliations; the study uses OpenAI models as agents and as monitors.
- Measured: whether a monitor that reads the chain of thought catches target behaviors better than one that sees only actions and outputs. Three evaluation types (intervention, process, outcome-property) and one metric, g-mean squared (true positive rate times true negative rate, comparable across behaviors with different base rates). Agents: GPT-5 Thinking, o3, o3-mini, o4-mini, plus Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Thinking), DeepSeek R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, Kimi K2 Thinking. Default monitor: GPT-5 Thinking at high effort; o3, o3-mini and o4-mini in the monitor-scaling tests.
- Key numbers: monitor scale, g-mean squared rose by nearly 40% going from o3-mini to GPT-5 Thinking as monitor (Section 6.3; unit is the paper's "%", relative versus points not stated). With o4-mini as monitor, going from lowest to highest reasoning effort improved monitorability by about 12% (Section 6.3). Qualitatively: action-only monitors scored low, CoT access generally helped a lot, longer chains were generally more monitorable, RL at current frontier scale did not materially reduce monitorability, and asking the agent follow-up questions and showing the follow-up reasoning to the monitor improved it. Exception: Claude 3.7 Sonnet had relatively high action-only monitorability but the lowest CoT monitorability of the models tested. The authors say it is unclear at what scale RL will start to hurt.
- Related (opened): Chen Yueh-Han, McCarthy, Lee, He, Kivlichan, Baker, Carroll, Korbak. "Reasoning Models Struggle to Control their Chains of Thought." arXiv 2603.05706, v1 5 Mar 2026, preprint. 13 reasoning models (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Sonnet 4, Sonnet 4.5; GPT-5.2, 5.1, 5; o4-mini, o3; GPT-OSS 120B and 20B; Qwen3 32B and 14B; DeepSeek R1). Task example: follow a rule such as "use only uppercase letters in your reasoning". Claude Sonnet 4.5 controlled its reasoning text 2.7% of the time versus 61.9% for its final answer. GPT-OSS-120B 5.9% versus 0.9% for the 20B; Qwen3 32B 2.3% versus 0.6% for the 14B. Control fell with more RL, more test-time compute and harder problems. Authors are cautiously optimistic and say the mechanism is not well understood.
- What an advanced user should do differently: when an agent run is long or costly to get wrong, keep the reasoning log and have a reviewer read it together with the actions; do not read actions alone. If the reviewer is a small model, give it more reasoning effort and let it ask the agent follow-up questions. Still treat the log as evidence, not as proof (manual 15.4 stays).
- Manual change: yes. Section 15.3 stops at Baker and Korbak. Add these two 2025-26 measurements, with the caveat that both are preprints, the first is mostly qualitative in its text, and both author groups say future scale or training could change the result.

## F03  A judge can be perfectly repeatable and still biased, and raw agreement overstates quality
- Source: Justin D. Norman, Michael U. Rivera, D. Alex Hughes. "Reliability without Validity: A Systematic, Large-Scale Evaluation of LLM-as-a-Judge Models Across Agreement, Consistency, and Bias." arXiv 2606.19544, v1 17 Jun 2026, cs.CL. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544
- Evidence class: preprint. No venue listed.
- Measured: 21 LLM judges from nine providers on MT-Bench, JudgeBench and RewardBench, three protocols (agreement, consistency, bias audit), 118 runs, about 541,000 judgments, run March-April 2026. One pairwise prompt template and fixed metric definitions for all judges (the authors list this as a limit). Position bias = how far the chance that "A wins" is from 0.5 over paired A-B and B-A runs.
- Key numbers: exact-match agreement exceeds chance-corrected (kappa) agreement by 33.8 to 41.3 percentage points across the 21 models (abstract says 33-41 on MT-Bench). Judge rankings moved by up to 14 places between benchmarks. Qwen 3 8B had test-retest 0.992 with position bias 0.192; Gemini 2.5 Flash 0.988 with 0.125. Verbosity bias was small (under 0.011) under that single rubric. Their short protocol: chance-correct, swap positions, replicate, cross-validate, audit the repeatability-versus-bias gap.
- Related (opened): Sadman Kabir Soumik. "Judging the Judges: A Systematic Evaluation of Bias Mitigation Strategies in LLM-as-a-Judge Pipelines." arXiv 2604.23178, v1 25 Apr 2026, v2 24 Jun 2026; the arXiv comments line says it is published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (2026) (I did not open the TMLR page). 9 debiasing strategies, 5 judges from 4 providers (Google, Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta), 3 benchmarks (MT-Bench n=400, LLMBar n=200, a custom set n=375), 4 bias types. Style bias (preferring markdown over plain prose) ranged 0.10 to 0.76 across models; position bias was at most 0.04 in that setup; length preference differed by family (Gemini Pro, Gemini Flash and Llama favored longer answers, +0.24 to +0.44; Claude favored shorter, -0.12; GPT-4o about neutral, -0.04). Best cost trade-off: Gemini 2.5 Flash with a "Combined Budget" strategy, 71.0% agreement (kappa 0.549) at about $0.001 per judgment, versus 69.5% at about $0.015 for Claude Sonnet 4.
- The two papers disagree on how large position bias is (at most 0.04 versus 0.125 to 0.192). They used different judges, prompts and benchmarks, so do not merge the numbers. The shared lesson is to measure your own judge.
- What an advanced user should do differently: (1) keep swapping A and B and call a flip a tie; (2) do not take repeatable scores as proof the judge is right; (3) check the judge against a small set of your own human-labeled cases and report chance-corrected agreement, not raw percent; (4) choose the judge on your task, because rankings move; (5) make the two answers look the same (same formatting) before judging, since style bias was the largest bias in the second paper; do not assume longer wins, because the direction depends on the model family.
- Manual change: yes, small. Section 13.3 rests on 2023 GPT-4 results. Add the repeatability-is-not-validity point, chance-corrected agreement, and format bias. The "different model family" rule is not tested head-on by the papers I opened, so I do not suggest changing it.

## F04  Agent set-up rule of thumb from a vendor, plus the best published residual attack rate
- Source: Meta AI. "Agents Rule of Two: A Practical Approach to AI Agent Security." Blog post, 31 Oct 2025. https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/
- Evidence class: vendor documentation (design guidance). The post contains no measured figures.
- Rule (paraphrased): until research can reliably detect and refuse prompt injection, an agent should have at most two of three properties in one session: (A) reads untrustworthy input, (B) can reach sensitive systems or private data, (C) can change state or communicate outside. If a task needs all three without starting a fresh context window, the agent should not run on its own; at minimum a human approval step or another reliable check is needed.
- Related (opened): Anthropic. "Mitigating the risk of prompt injections in browser use." 24 Nov 2025. Vendor documentation. The page calls a 1% attack success rate a real improvement that still carries meaningful risk, and says prompt injection is far from solved and no browser agent is immune. The 1% comes from Anthropic's internal adaptive "Best-of-N" attacker, 100 attempts per environment, comparing the launch browser-extension version with the original launch setup. The sentence does not name the model, and the per-version values are in a chart image that I could not read. So the 1% is the vendor's own figure on its own test, not an independent measurement.
- What an advanced user should do differently: before connecting an agent (browser, email, coding) to your accounts, count which of the three properties it has. If it reads web pages, emails or shared files, remove either access to private data or the ability to act and send, or require your approval for each state-changing step, or split the job into separate sessions. A 1% rate under the vendor's own adaptive test is not a safe level for irreversible actions repeated across many pages (my inference).
- Manual change: yes, small. Section 17.3's design rule (least privilege, out-of-model authorization) matches this; add the Rule of Two as a short checklist form, labeled vendor guidance and not a proven defense. Add the Anthropic page as a deployed-agent data point supporting 17.1's "no foolproof prevention". No existing manual statement is contradicted.

## Practical takeaways for prompting today
1. Do not ask a model to recheck its own answer in the same chat. Present the draft as someone else's text, use a new chat, or use a reasoning model. Source: Tsui 2025 (arXiv 2507.02778); Chen et al. 2026 (arXiv 2606.05976).
2. If you use a model as a judge, swap the order and treat flips as ties, and compare it with a small set of your own human-labeled cases using chance-corrected agreement. Make both answers look the same in format. Source: Norman et al. 2026 (arXiv 2606.19544); Soumik 2026 (arXiv 2604.23178).
3. For long agent runs, keep the reasoning log and have a reviewer read it with the actions. A reviewer that reads actions only did worse in that study. The log is evidence, not proof. Source: Guan et al. 2025 (arXiv 2512.18311); Chen Yueh-Han et al. 2026 (arXiv 2603.05706).
4. Give an agent no more than two of: untrusted input, private data access, ability to act outside. Require human approval if you need all three. Source: Meta, Agents Rule of Two (31 Oct 2025).
5. Plan for a residual attack rate even with the best vendor defenses (about 1% on Anthropic's own adaptive test), so keep irreversible actions behind approval. Source: Anthropic, 24 Nov 2025.
